# Introduction to Three-Statement Financial Modelling

## Overview

A **three-statement financial model** connects three core financial statements:

1. Income Statement
2. Balance Sheet
3. Cash Flow Statement

Businesses use this type of model to understand historical financial performance and forecast future financial results.

It is commonly used in financial planning, corporate finance, investment analysis and business valuation.

## Why Is Three-Statement Modelling Important?

A three-statement model helps businesses:

- Forecast revenue, costs and profitability.
- Understand their financial position.
- Monitor cash inflows and outflows.
- Assess future funding requirements.
- Evaluate business performance.
- Understand how changes in one financial statement affect the others.

The statements are connected, so assumptions made in one part of the model can affect the remaining statements.

## The Three Financial Statements

### 1. Income Statement

The income statement, also known as the **Profit and Loss Statement (P&L)**, reports a company's financial performance over a specific period.

It includes revenue and expenses and shows whether the business generated a profit or loss.

Typical components include:

- Revenue
- Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
- Gross Profit
- Operating Expenses
- Operating Profit
- Interest Expense
- Tax Expense
- Net Profit

The basic relationship is:

**Profit = Revenue − Expenses**

The exact structure depends on the business and the level of detail in the model.

### 2. Balance Sheet

The balance sheet provides a snapshot of a company's financial position at a particular point in time.

It shows three main categories:

- Assets
- Liabilities
- Shareholders' Equity

The fundamental accounting equation is:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

Assets represent resources owned or controlled by the business.

Liabilities represent obligations owed to other parties.

Equity represents the residual interest in the business after liabilities are deducted from assets.

Unlike the income statement, which covers a period, the balance sheet represents a position at a specific date.

### 3. Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement shows how cash enters and leaves a business over a specified period.

It is generally divided into three categories:

- **Operating Activities:** Cash flows associated with the business's main operations.
- **Investing Activities:** Cash flows associated with investments in assets and other investments.
- **Financing Activities:** Cash flows associated with borrowing, repaying debt, issuing shares and returning capital to shareholders.

The cash flow statement helps explain why a business's cash balance changes over time.

Net income is often used as a starting point for the operating cash flow section under the indirect method. Adjustments are then made for non-cash items and changes in working capital.

## How the Three Statements Connect

The three statements are linked through several important relationships.

### Income Statement to Cash Flow Statement

Net income is often the starting point for calculating operating cash flow under the indirect method.

Adjustments may include:

- Depreciation and amortisation
- Changes in accounts receivable
- Changes in inventory
- Changes in accounts payable
- Other non-cash items

These adjustments help convert accounting profit into operating cash flow.

### Income Statement to Balance Sheet

Net income contributes to retained earnings through the closing process, after accounting for dividends and other relevant adjustments.

Expenses and revenue can also affect balance sheet accounts through accruals and working capital movements.

### Balance Sheet to Cash Flow Statement

Changes in balance sheet accounts help explain cash movements.

For example:

- An increase in accounts receivable can reduce operating cash flow relative to reported revenue.
- An increase in accounts payable can increase operating cash flow relative to expenses recognised.
- Purchasing equipment affects cash and property, plant and equipment.

A complete three-statement model links these relationships through formulas and assumptions.

## Preparing to Build the Model

Before creating a three-statement financial model, gather the information required for the forecast.

This may include:

- Historical financial statements
- Revenue assumptions
- Cost assumptions
- Operating expense assumptions
- Tax assumptions
- Capital expenditure forecasts
- Working capital assumptions
- Debt and interest assumptions
- Depreciation assumptions

The assumptions should be clearly documented so that users can understand how the model works.

## Common Financial Modelling Practices

### Separate Assumptions from Formulas

Financial models often use different formatting to distinguish hard-coded inputs from calculated values.

For example, a model may use:

- Blue font for hard-coded inputs.
- Black font for formulas.
- Green font for references to other worksheets.

These are conventions rather than universal rules. Follow the formatting standards used by your organisation or workbook.

### Use Clear Labels

Each row and column should have a meaningful heading.

Include the relevant financial period and units, such as pounds, thousands of pounds or percentages.

### Use Consistent Time Periods

Ensure that revenue, expenses, cash flow and balance sheet assumptions are aligned to the correct periods.

For example, monthly revenue assumptions should not be mixed with annual expense assumptions without appropriate adjustments.

### Use Formulas Where Possible

Link calculated values to the relevant assumptions or financial statement line items rather than repeatedly typing the same numbers.

This makes the model easier to update and reduces the risk of inconsistent calculations.

## Key Takeaways

- A three-statement model combines the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement.
- The income statement reports financial performance over a period.
- The balance sheet shows financial position at a specific date.
- The cash flow statement explains cash movements over a period.
- The three statements are connected through accounting relationships.
- Accurate assumptions and consistent formulas are essential for reliable forecasts.

Use three-statement modelling when forecasting a company's financial performance, analysing its future financial position or assessing how operating and financing decisions affect profitability and cash flow.
