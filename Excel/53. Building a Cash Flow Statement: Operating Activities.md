# Building a Cash Flow Statement: Operating Activities

## Overview

A cash flow statement explains how cash moves into and out of a business during a period.

It is commonly divided into three sections:

1. Operating activities
2. Investing activities
3. Financing activities

This note focuses on operating cash flow using the indirect method.

## 1. Link Net Income

The cash flow statement begins with net income from the income statement.

For example:

`='Income Statement'!B35`

Use the actual worksheet name and cell reference from your workbook.

Copy the formula across the forecast years.

## 2. Add Back Depreciation and Amortisation

Depreciation and amortisation reduce accounting profit but do not normally represent cash payments in the current period.

Under the indirect method, they are added back to net income.

If total depreciation and amortisation is in cell `C13` on the CapEx and depreciation schedule, enter:

`='CapEx and Depreciation Schedule'!C13`

Copy the formula across the forecast years.

## 3. Calculate the Change in Accounts Receivable

An increase in accounts receivable generally reduces operating cash flow because more revenue has been recognised than cash collected.

For the first forecast year, suppose:
- Opening accounts receivable is in `B6` on the balance sheet.
- Closing accounts receivable is in `C6`.

Enter:

`=B6-C6`

This formula calculates the cash flow adjustment from the change in accounts receivable.

If accounts receivable increases, the result is negative.

If accounts receivable decreases, the result is positive.

## 4. Calculate the Change in Accounts Payable

An increase in accounts payable generally increases operating cash flow because the business has incurred expenses without yet paying the suppliers.

Suppose:
- Opening accounts payable is in `B17` on the balance sheet.
- Closing accounts payable is in `C17`.

Enter:

`=C17-B17`

If accounts payable increases, the result is positive.

If accounts payable decreases, the result is negative.

## 5. Calculate the Change in Deferred Revenue

An increase in deferred revenue generally increases operating cash flow because the business has received payment before recognising the related revenue.

If deferred revenue is in row 18 on the balance sheet, use:

`=C18-B18`

This calculates the change between the closing and opening balances.

Check the sign convention and ensure the formula references the correct periods.

## 6. Calculate Cash Flow from Operating Activities

Under the indirect method, operating cash flow begins with net income and adjusts for non-cash expenses and changes in operating working capital.

A simplified formula is:

Operating Cash Flow = Net Income + Depreciation and Amortisation + Working Capital Adjustments

If:
- Net income is in `B5`.
- Depreciation and amortisation are in `B6`.
- The change in accounts receivable is in `B7`.
- The change in accounts payable is in `B8`.
- The change in deferred revenue is in `B9`.

Enter:

`=B5+SUM(B6:B9)`

Copy the formula across the forecast years.

If the workbook includes additional operating cash flow adjustments, include those as well.

## Key Takeaways

- Operating cash flow shows the cash generated or used by normal business activities.
- Net income is the starting point under the indirect method.
- Depreciation and amortisation are added back as non-cash expenses.
- Increases in accounts receivable generally reduce operating cash flow.
- Increases in accounts payable and deferred revenue generally increase operating cash flow.
- All relevant working capital accounts must be included for the cash flow statement to be complete.
